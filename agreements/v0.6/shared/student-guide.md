# Student Guide to Course Project Agreements

Draft v0.6. Not binding. Not legal advice.

This guide explains the choices a course project makes about information and intellectual property ("IP"). It also explains what each choice means for you when you publish, seek a patent, or start a company. You must confirm that you have read it before you choose a project.

This guide explains. It doesn't bind anyone, and it isn't legal advice. The binding terms are in the course-level document that everyone in the Course signs, and in your project's agreement. (A team without a partner records its choices in a short written election instead.) If this guide and an agreement differ, the agreement controls. For advice about your own situation, ask the instructor, Harvard's Office of Technology Development (OTD), or your own lawyer.

## Your choices at a glance

Each project makes one information choice and one ownership choice. When students own the work, the project also picks a public license for software, and one for everything else (documentation, reports, data, and media). If a choice is blank, or more than one is checked (other than MIT and Apache 2.0 together), the default applies.

**Open Science** is the default. It means Open information, Students own, MIT and/or Apache 2.0 for software, and CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-SA 4.0 for everything else. Any other choice needs the instructor's approval in writing (email is enough), at signing and for any later change. A partner certifies that approval on the project's form. Without a partner, the students certify it, and an individual project always needs it.

| Choice | What it means for you | Open Science? |

Three more points:

- **Partner's license.** When students own, a partner's license is permanent and royalty-free. It moves only with Partner's business, and Partner can't sublicense it without the students' consent.
- **Publication review.** Partner may request it unless information is Open, and it applies automatically when Partner owns. You send Partner a copy 30 days before you submit, then wait for the hold period on the form (if blank, until Partner consents). Partner may not unreasonably withhold consent for results with no Confidential Information. If Partner owns and information is Open, there's no review. Instead, you give 30 days' notice before a public disclosure, and Partner may delay it up to 60 days to file a patent.
- **Saying no.** You accept a project's terms before you start work on it. You may ask for an Open Science project or a project without a partner. If you decline a project's terms, the Course Staff will reassign you on request. Work you already did stays under the first project's terms, and its partner's information stays confidential.

## Copyright

### What it protects, and who owns it

Copyright protects original expression, such as code, documentation, reports, and figures. It doesn't protect ideas, algorithms, or methods as such; a patent may. In the U.S., copyright arises automatically once a work is fixed, for example saved to a file. Registration is optional, but you need it before suing over a U.S. work. Statutory damages and attorney's fees also generally require timely registration.

Each person who contributes original expression is an author. Teammates who write together, intending their parts to merge, usually own a "joint work" together. Under U.S. law, absent an agreement, each co-owner may use the work, and grant non-exclusive licenses, without the others' consent. But each must share profits with the others. An exclusive license, or a transfer of the whole work, needs every co-owner.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm this summary (v0.5 review note R-7): 17 U.S.C. § 201(a), and *Davis v. Blige*, 505 F.3d 90 (2d Cir. 2007). Please also confirm whether the teammates' royalty-free licenses displace the duty to account, and say so in the agreements if they should. Rules outside the U.S. differ.

Your project's choices change these defaults: teammates license each other, a partner gets a license, or a public license covers everyone. Anyone outside the team who writes code for you may own it. The course-wide rules bar sharing or using anyone else's confidential information, such as a current or former employer's. Open-source code you use keeps its own license.

### AI-generated code and documentation

U.S. copyright requires a human author. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter* (2025), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit upheld the refusal to register an image that a machine made on its own. The Supreme Court declined to review the case in March 2026. The court left open how much human contribution is enough when a person uses AI as a tool.

The U.S. Copyright Office gave its view in Part 2 of its report on copyright and AI (January 2025):

- Prompts alone are generally not enough with current tools, because the user doesn't control the expressive choices.
- Your own expression is protected where it is perceptible in the output.
- Creative selection, arrangement, and modification of AI output can be protected.
- Using AI to assist your own work doesn't by itself undermine protection. Each case turns on its facts.

What this means for you:

- Code you write, or meaningfully edit, can be protected. Large blocks generated from prompts and accepted as is may have thin protection, or none. Then contracts, confidentiality, and licenses matter more.
- To register a copyright, you must disclose and exclude AI-generated material that is more than minimal. Don't list an AI tool as an author.
- Keep a record of what you wrote and what you generated, such as commit history. It helps you show authorship later.
- AI output can reproduce other people's code, including open-source code with license conditions. Review it as you would code copied from the web.
- Each tool's terms say who owns its output and how it uses your inputs. The course-wide rules say to use Harvard accounts and tools by default. A partner's restricted information may go only into tools that partner approves in writing or by email.

> **Note for counsel:** the law here is moving. This section reflects *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, No. 23-5233 (D.C. Cir. Mar. 18, 2025), cert. denied (U.S. Mar. 2, 2026); the Copyright Office's Part 2 report (January 2025); and its registration guidance (March 16, 2023). Pending cases, such as *Allen v. Perlmutter* (D. Colo.), may change it. Other countries differ. Recheck before each use.

## Patents and public disclosure

### Inventors, AI, and co-owners

A patent protects a new, useful, and non-obvious invention, which can include a software method. It exists only if the patent office grants an application. Inventors are the people who contributed to conceiving the invention as claimed. The attorney who files the application determines inventorship under a strict legal standard, and will consider each teammate's and partner's contribution.

An AI tool can't be an inventor: an inventor must be a natural person (*Thaler v. Vidal*, Fed. Cir. 2022). In November 2025, the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) withdrew its 2024 guidance on AI-assisted inventions. It now applies the usual standard to every invention and treats AI as a tool. The question is whether a person conceived the claimed invention.

In the U.S., absent an agreement, each co-owner of a patent may make, use, and sell the invention without the others' consent, and without sharing profits (35 U.S.C. § 262). Each may also license it non-exclusively. But all co-owners generally must join a lawsuit to enforce the patent, so one co-owner can block enforcement. Other countries vary. In some, co-owners need each other's consent to license or even use the patent.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm (v0.5 review note R-2): 35 U.S.C. § 262; the joinder rule (*Ethicon, Inc. v. U.S. Surgical Corp.*, 135 F.3d 1456 (Fed. Cir. 1998); *STC.UNM v. Intel Corp.*, 754 F.3d 940 (Fed. Cir. 2014)); the filing attorney's role in inventorship; and the statement about other countries.

### Public disclosure

Disclosing an invention before you file can cost you patent rights. Disclosures include papers, preprints, public code, talks, posters, demos, and pitches to investors or competition judges, whenever the audience isn't bound to confidentiality. A poster shown at a meeting can be a "printed publication" even if no copies are handed out (*In re Klopfenstein*, Fed. Cir. 2004). Treat course events as public, because not every attendee must keep what they see confidential. Publishing work under a public license is a public disclosure.

**The U.S. grace period.** Your own disclosure isn't prior art against you if you file within one year after it (35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1)). It's a safety net, not a plan. Someone else's earlier disclosure or filing can still count against you, and the grace period doesn't help abroad.

**Absolute novelty elsewhere.** Many countries require absolute novelty. Under the European Patent Convention, an invention isn't new if it was made available to the public, in any way, before the filing date (or the priority date). The exceptions are narrow: an evident abuse against you, or display at certain official international exhibitions, within six months before filing. So a course poster or a public repository can bar a European patent. Some countries, such as Japan, South Korea, and Canada, have their own grace periods, with different rules.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm the grace-period summary. Sources found while drafting describe 12-month grace periods in Japan (since 2018), South Korea, and Canada, with differing conditions. Check current national law.

### Before you file

If your work may be patentable, talk to your teammates, any partner, and the Course Staff before any course event. Until an application is filed:

- **Show** what the invention does: the problem, who it helps, and results such as accuracy, speed, or cost.
- **Leave out** how it works: architecture, algorithms, pseudocode, equations, key parameters, training recipes, data processing, and source code.
- Don't post the code publicly, post a preprint, or pitch in public.
- Take care with demos that let people see or infer how it works.

Results alone can sometimes reveal the method, so ask when in doubt.

A U.S. provisional application is a common first step. It isn't examined, and it expires after 12 months. To keep its filing date, you must file a regular (nonprovisional) application within those 12 months. Under the Paris Convention, you also have 12 months from your first filing to file in other member countries and keep the earlier date (called "priority"). A provisional secures that date only for what it describes, so describe the invention fully. If Partner owns, Partner decides whether to file. If Harvard has rights, OTD decides. Otherwise, the owners decide and pay.

## Startups when teammates share ownership

### Where deadlocks arise

Deadlocks arise when students own work that has no public license, including when a team without a partner keeps its rights. The co-ownership rules above then apply. No single teammate can give a company exclusive rights, and a teammate who has moved on may not respond. Under a public license, FSL, or Partner owns, the license or the agreement already settles who may do what.

### Teammates' licenses and Student Companies

Whenever students own or co-own the work, with or without a partner, the agreements provide:

- Each student gives each teammate a perpetual, non-exclusive, royalty-free license to the team's work.
- Your license extends to your Student Company. That's a company you founded or co-founded, or one in which students on the project together hold at least 25% of the voting equity. For a partner project, no competitor of the partner may control it.
- Course work stays non-exclusive. What your company builds afterwards is its own.
- Teammates who start separate companies each keep their licenses.

So any teammate can build on the work without asking, but no one gets exclusive rights in it.

### The buyout ("use it or lose it")

The buyout applies only where students own work with no public license, in software or any other category. It doesn't apply under joint ownership with FSL, or when Partner owns.

1. A Student Company that is actively developing the work gives written notice to a teammate who isn't using it commercially, personally or through a company.
2. The teammate may join the company within [60] days, on the same terms as its other student founders.
3. If not, the teammate must assign their ownership share to the company, for a price.

One of two prices will be chosen before the agreements are used:

- **[Alternative 1]** The greater of (a) what each founding student received for assigning their own rights in the work to the company, and (b) [0.01]% of the company's fully diluted equity. It's paid at the buyout and may be in equity, so its value can follow the company's.
- **[Alternative 2]** [__]% of the proceeds of any sale of the company, or of substantially all its assets, up to $[__], paid at the sale. It pays only if a sale happens, and gives no equity to someone who isn't a founder.

Also still open: [whether the sale ends the seller's license]. If it does, the seller may still use the work for portfolio, academic, and other noncommercial purposes. If it doesn't, the company can't get exclusive rights.

### Why investors want exclusive rights

Investors back companies whose products others can't easily copy. Licenses held by teammates or a partner shrink that edge. So investors usually ask founders to assign their IP to the company, and check that no one else owns a share.

Patents raise the stakes, because a patent's value is the right to exclude others. A co-owner outside the company can license competitors, and can block enforcement by refusing to join a lawsuit.

Talk with your teammates early, and write down what you agree. The instructor can point you to help, including patent attorneys who hold office hours for students, and the Harvard Innovation Labs.

## Startups on openly licensed work

### Why it's usually easier

With a public license, there's nothing to deadlock. Every teammate, any partner, and everyone else may already use, change, and sell the work, so no one's consent is needed. Your company's value comes from what you add: new code, data, customers, and execution. You may license your later work as you choose, if you keep the original notices.

### Caveats

- **Others share the base.** Competitors and Partner can build on the same code.
- **Publishing is a public disclosure.** If anyone wants a patent, file before you publish.
- **Apache 2.0 licenses patents; MIT is silent.** Under Apache 2.0, each contributor licenses everyone under the contributor's patent claims that their contribution necessarily infringes. A recipient who sues, claiming the work infringes a patent, loses that license. So if you later patent what your Apache-licensed code does, its users may already be licensed. MIT doesn't mention patents, and whether it implies a patent license is unsettled.
- **Keep the notices.** MIT requires its copyright and permission notice in all copies or substantial portions. Apache 2.0 requires a copy of the license, notices in changed files, and keeping existing notices, including any NOTICE file. CC BY requires credit and a license notice. CC BY-SA also requires sharing adaptations under the same or a compatible license.
- **The project's name isn't licensed.** Apache 2.0 expressly grants no rights in trade names or trademarks, and Creative Commons licenses exclude trademarks. MIT doesn't mention them. Pick your own company name, and check that it's free.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm that whether the MIT License implies a patent license is unsettled. Please also confirm how dual licensing affects the Apache 2.0 patent license for a recipient who chooses MIT.

### FSL: a middle path

With a partner, the Functional Source License (FSL) is available only when Students and Partner own the work jointly. A team without a partner may also choose it. It suits a team that expects to commercialize. Anyone may use FSL code for any purpose except a "Competing Use." That means offering it in a commercial product or service that substitutes for the software, or for another product the owners offer using it. It also means offering the same or substantially similar functionality. Each version becomes available under MIT or Apache 2.0 two years after it is released.

Under the agreements, any partner and each student may use the code for any purpose, including through a Student Company, and that isn't a Competing Use. No owner may license it on other terms without amending the agreement, and no owner owes the others a share of profits.

Weigh these points:

- Competitors are barred for two years, but your teammates and any partner aren't.
- After two years, each version is open to everyone, competitors included.
- Until then, FSL code is source-available, not open source, and some users avoid it.
- Releasing FSL code is still a public disclosure, and the buyout doesn't apply.

## Harvard's IP policy

Harvard's "Statement of Policy in Regard to Intellectual Property" applies to students. It covers anyone who uses Harvard funds, facilities, or other resources, or who works under an agreement between Harvard and a third party.

Ownership depends on how the work was made, not on who made it. Harvard owns inventions, software, and databases made with Harvard-administered funding, with more than incidental use of Harvard resources, or under a third-party agreement. If you invent with only incidental use, such as classrooms and instructional labs in ordinary course participation, you own the invention. Harvard keeps a limited right to use it for research and education.

### OTD's informal exception for coursework

OTD has created an informal exception for coursework. Harvard doesn't claim ownership of your rights in IP from your work in the Course, provided that:

- the Course isn't funded by, or conducted under, an agreement between Harvard and a third party; and
- the only Harvard-administered resources you use are those provided generally to everyone in the Course.

The instructor can tell you whether the exception applies to your Course.

### When it may not apply

- **Third-party agreements.** If a sponsor funds the Course for research, or provides enabling resources in exchange for IP rights, Harvard may own course inventions. You'd usually be asked to sign Harvard's Participation Agreement first.
- **Other Harvard resources.** Research computing, lab equipment, a research group's data, or Harvard-administered money can bring your work under the policy, even money spent only on supplies or computing fees. Ask the instructor first.
- **Research.** Harvard's rights from faculty-sponsored research come first. Ask before bringing lab work into your project.
- **People outside the team.** Someone who isn't in the Course may owe their work to an employer.

Please confirm the treatment of incidental inventions, including Harvard's reserved right, under the current policy. Please also confirm the exception's wording with OTD; the drafter found no published statement of it.
