# Student Guide to Course Project Agreements

Draft v0.6.

This guide explains the choices a course project makes about information and intellectual property ("IP"). It also explains what each choice means when you show your work, seek a patent, or start a company. You must confirm that you have read it before you choose a project.

This guide explains the agreements, but it doesn't bind anyone, and it isn't legal advice. The binding terms are in the course-level document that everyone in the Course signs, and in your project's agreement. (A project without a partner records its choices on a short form.) If this guide and an agreement differ, the agreement controls. For questions about the Course, ask the instructor. For questions about Harvard's rights, ask Harvard's Office of Technology Development (OTD), which acts for Harvard. For advice about your own situation, ask your own lawyer.

## Your choices at a glance

Each project makes one information choice and one ownership choice. Unless Partner owns, it also picks a license for software, and one for everything else (documentation, reports, data, and media). If a choice is blank, or more than one is checked (other than MIT and Apache 2.0 together), the default applies.

**Open Science** is the default. It combines Open information, student ownership (or a public domain dedication under CC0), MIT and/or Apache 2.0 for software, and CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-SA 4.0 for everything else. Every license you grant under the agreements is irrevocable. Any other choice needs the instructor's approval in writing (email is enough), at signing and for any later change. A partner certifies that approval on the project's form.

More points to know:

- **Partner's license.** When students own, Partner gets a perpetual, non-exclusive, royalty-free license to use the work for any purpose. It moves only with Partner's business, and Partner can't sublicense it without the students' consent. Under a public license, Partner may also use the work like anyone else.
- **Publication review.** Unless information is Open, Partner may elect it, and it always applies when Partner owns. Send Partner a copy at least 30 days before you submit. Then wait until Partner consents or the hold period on the form ends, counted from when Partner gets the copy (if blank, until Partner consents). Partner may not unreasonably withhold consent for results with no Confidential Information.
- **Patent hold.** If Partner owns and information is Open, there's no review. Instead, give Partner 30 days' notice before a public disclosure. Partner may delay it until up to 60 days after your notice, to file a patent.
- **Claims against Partner.** You and Partner release each other from claims about the Course and the project, except for a breach of the project's agreement. Partner isn't otherwise liable to students or the Course Staff. (Whether the same applies to Harvard is a question for counsel.) Other exceptions, for example for intellectual property or personal-injury claims, are still to be settled.
- **Changing a choice** needs a written amendment signed for Partner and by every student still on the project, plus the instructor's approval if the result isn't Open Science.
- **Saying no.** You must accept a project's terms before starting work on it. You may ask to move to another project, such as an Open Science project or one without a partner. The Course Staff will try in good faith to accommodate you, and will always move you to an Open Science project if you ask, though not necessarily to a particular one. Work already done stays under the first project's terms, and its partner's information stays confidential. Even if the Course is required for your degree, you can always ask for an Open Science project, so you never have to accept a Partner's nondisclosure terms or assign your intellectual property.

**Projects without a partner** need the instructor's permission, and make similar choices, with the students in place of a partner. Open, Course Private, and Confidential isn't available. A team may choose FSL, with a representative chosen by majority. The students certify the instructor's approval for anything other than Open Science. An individual project, with or without a partner, always needs the instructor's approval; with a partner, the partner certifies it.

### What you can do with the work afterwards

- **Students own, with public licenses:** everyone may use the work under those licenses.
- **Students own, no public license:** you may use the work for any purpose, including through your Student Company, under your teammates' licenses. Partner keeps its license. No one has exclusive rights, so see "Planning ahead" below.
- **Students and Partner own jointly:** you, your teammates, and Partner may each use the code for any purpose, including through a Student Company. Everyone else may use it under FSL.
- **Partner owns:** you may use the work for demonstrations, your portfolio, and the final poster and video, subject to confidentiality. The agreements give no one a right to buy the work if Partner leaves it unused (see "Planning ahead" below).
- **In every case:** Partner's own data and code stay Partner's. You may use them only for the project during the Course, apart from the demo data described under "Showing your work."

The agreements say that no teammate owes another a share of profits from using the work.

## Showing your work

- **In the Course.** You may use the project in coursework, class presentations, and poster and video sessions without Partner's consent. Mask Partner's name and logos with generic names, such as "Investment Bank." Give Partner a copy by the time you present or submit, and make reasonable edits it asks for.
- **Final poster and video.** You may show them to employers, graduate programs, and similar audiences. With Open information, you may publish them once Partner's edits are made, still masked. Otherwise, you need Partner's written consent to hand out copies or links.
- **Demonstrations.** You may demonstrate working software live to the same audiences. Mask Partner's name, reveal no Confidential Information, and hand out no copies. With Partner's consent, which it may not unreasonably refuse, you may share a demo video privately, such as on a password-protected site.
- **Partner's data in demos.** Partner identifies in writing limited or simulated data or code that you may use in noncommercial demonstrations, for the Confidentiality Period. You may demonstrate a model trained on Partner's data, but not extract or disclose the data.
- **Credit.** Before anyone submits a publication about the work, teammates and Partner's contributors are offered co-authorship or acknowledgment, and time to review the draft.
- **Patents.** For patents, treat every course event as public.

## Rules for everyone in the Course

These rules bind every student and every member of the Course Staff, and protect every project. Partners in the Course may enforce the rules that protect their information.

- **The Course Community** is the Course's students and Course Staff, Harvard staff who support the Course, and [SEAS community members and] invited guests at private Course events.
- **Mistakes.** If you receive confidential information not meant for you, use reasonable care to tell the sender, not use or copy it, and delete it. Doing this promptly is reasonable care. No one is liable just for receiving it, but later use isn't protected.
- **Other projects' information.** Keep other projects' Course Private Information within the Course Community. Never pass it to a partner without written consent from that project's partner and its students (or, for a project without a partner, its students).
- **Others' confidential information.** Don't share, or use in a project, anyone else's confidential information, such as a current or former employer's. Code you wrote for a job usually belongs to your employer, so don't reuse it without permission. Your employment agreement may also cover work you do outside your job.
- **Generative AI.** Use your Harvard accounts and Harvard's tools by default. A partner's restricted information may go only into tools that partner approves in writing or by email. Read each tool's terms on who owns its output and how it uses your inputs.
- **Course Staff** contribute no IP, and share no grades or other education records with partners unless you ask in writing. They see a project's Confidential Information only by signing its agreement. No student may be a project's partner[ without Harvard's approval], and no instructor without Harvard's consent. Any other member of the Course Staff may be one only if they don't evaluate that project's work or see its students' grades.
- **Notices.** Notices under the agreements go to your Harvard and personal emails, with a text or voicemail. Each notice is sent three times, about [7] days apart, unless you acknowledge it sooner. It counts from the day it was first sent, even if you don't answer, once a text or voicemail has reached your phone. If you gave no phone number, or yours no longer works and you didn't report a change, it counts without one. Notices you send, such as a copy for publication review, follow the same rule. Keep your personal email and phone current with your project's other signers (including any partner) and any system the Course provides for the agreements, and, during the Course, with the Course Staff.
- **How long.** Confidentiality duties, including for other projects' information, last five years after your End Date (the Confidentiality Period). Your End Date is the end of the Course, or the day you stop taking part, if earlier. Trade secrets stay protected while they remain trade secrets.

## Copyright

### What it protects, and who owns it

Copyright protects original expression, such as code, documentation, reports, and figures. It doesn't protect ideas, algorithms, or methods as such, though a patent may. In the U.S., copyright arises automatically once a work is fixed, for example saved to a file. Registration is optional, but you need it to sue over a U.S. work.

Each person who contributes original expression is an author. Teammates who write together, intending their parts to merge, usually own a "joint work" together. Under U.S. law, absent an agreement, each co-owner may use the work, and grant non-exclusive licenses, without the others' consent. A co-owner who profits may owe the others a share. An exclusive license, or a transfer of the whole work, needs every co-owner. The agreements change these defaults, as described above.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm this summary (v0.5 review note R-7; 17 U.S.C. § 201(a); *Davis v. Blige*, 505 F.3d 90 (2d Cir. 2007)). Rules outside the U.S. differ.

Anyone outside the team who writes code for you may own it. Open-source code you use keeps its own license.

### AI-generated code and documentation

U.S. copyright requires a human author. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter* (D.C. Cir. 2025), the court upheld the refusal to register an image that a machine made on its own. The Supreme Court declined review in March 2026. The court left open how much human contribution is enough when a person uses AI as a tool. The U.S. Copyright Office's report on copyrightability (January 2025) gives its view:

- Prompts alone are generally not enough with current tools, because the user doesn't control the expressive choices.
- Your own expression that is perceptible in the output can be protected, and so can creative selection, arrangement, or modification of AI output.
- Using AI to assist your own work doesn't by itself undermine protection. Each case turns on its facts.

What this means for you:

- Code generated from prompts and accepted as is generally isn't protected by itself. Your own code and creative edits can be. Where protection is thin, contracts, confidentiality, and licenses matter more.
- To register a copyright, you must disclose and exclude AI-generated material that is more than minimal.
- Keep a record, such as commit history, of what you wrote and what you generated.
- AI output can reproduce other people's code, including open-source code with license conditions. Review it as you would code copied from the web.

> **Note for counsel:** the law here is moving. This reflects *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, No. 23-5233 (D.C. Cir. 2025), cert. denied (Mar. 2, 2026); the Copyright Office's Part 2 report (2025); and its registration guidance (2023). Pending cases, such as *Allen v. Perlmutter* (D. Colo.), may change it. Recheck before each use.

## Patents and public disclosure

### Inventors, AI, and co-owners

A patent protects a new, useful, and non-obvious invention, and some software methods qualify. But an abstract idea, such as a bare algorithm, isn't patentable just because it runs on a computer (*Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank*, 2014). A patent exists only once the patent office grants it.

Inventors are the people who contributed to conceiving the invention as claimed. The attorney who files the application makes a good-faith determination of inventorship under a strict legal standard, considering each contributor. An AI tool can't be an inventor, because an inventor must be a natural person (*Thaler v. Vidal*, Fed. Cir. 2022). Current USPTO guidance (November 2025) applies the usual inventorship standard to AI-assisted inventions, treating AI as a tool.

In the U.S., absent an agreement, each co-owner of a patent may make, use, offer to sell, sell, and import the invention (35 U.S.C. § 262). No co-owner needs the others' consent or owes them a share of profits, and each may license the patent non-exclusively. But all co-owners generally must join a lawsuit to enforce the patent, so one co-owner can block enforcement. Other countries vary; some require co-owners' consent to license or even use the patent.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm (v0.5 review note R-2): 35 U.S.C. § 262; the joinder rule (*Ethicon, Inc. v. U.S. Surgical Corp.*, 135 F.3d 1456 (Fed. Cir. 1998); *STC.UNM v. Intel Corp.*, 754 F.3d 940 (Fed. Cir. 2014)); the filing attorney's role in inventorship; and the statement about other countries.

### Public disclosure

Disclosing an invention before you file can cost you patent rights. Disclosures include papers, preprints, public code, talks, posters, demos, and pitches, whenever the audience isn't bound to confidentiality. A poster shown at a meeting can be a "printed publication," even with no copies handed out (*In re Klopfenstein*, Fed. Cir. 2004). Treat course events as public, and remember that publishing work under a public license is a public disclosure.

**The U.S. grace period.** Your own disclosure isn't prior art against you if you file within one year (35 U.S.C. § 102(b)(1)). Nor is a disclosure by someone who got the invention from you. It's a safety net, not a plan. Someone else's earlier disclosure or filing can still count against you, and it doesn't help abroad.

**Absolute novelty elsewhere.** Under the European Patent Convention, an invention isn't new if it was made public in any way before the filing (or priority) date. The only exceptions are an evident abuse against you, or display at certain official international exhibitions, within six months before filing. So a course poster or a public repository can bar a European patent. Japan, South Korea, Canada, and some other countries have grace periods, with different rules.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm the grace-period summary. Sources found while drafting describe 12-month grace periods in Japan (since 2018), South Korea, and Canada, with differing conditions. Check current national law.

### Before you file

If your work may be patentable, talk to your teammates, any partner, and the Course Staff before any course event. If Harvard may have rights in it, report it to OTD well before any public disclosure. Until an application is filed:

- **Show** what the invention does: the problem, who it helps, and results such as accuracy, speed, or cost.
- **Leave out** how it works: architecture, algorithms, pseudocode, equations, key parameters, training recipes, data processing, and source code.
- Don't post the code publicly, post a preprint, or pitch in public.
- Take care with demos that let people see or infer how it works. Results alone can sometimes reveal the method, so ask when in doubt.

A U.S. provisional application is a common first step. It isn't examined, and to keep its filing date you must file a regular application within 12 months. Under the Paris Convention, you also have 12 months to file in other member countries and keep your first filing date (called "priority"). A provisional secures that date only for what it describes, so describe the invention fully.

If Partner owns, Partner decides whether to file, and you must sign the papers it reasonably needs, at its expense. If you don't within [15] business days, Partner may sign them in your name. If Harvard has rights, OTD decides. Otherwise, the owners decide and pay.

## Startups when teammates share ownership

### Where deadlocks arise

Deadlocks arise when students own work that has no public license. The co-ownership rules then apply. No single teammate can give a company exclusive rights, and a teammate who has moved on may not respond. Under a public license, under FSL, or when Partner owns, the license or the agreement already settles who may do what.

### Teammates' licenses and Student Companies

Whenever students own or co-own the work, with or without a partner, the agreements provide:

- Each student gives each teammate a perpetual, non-exclusive, royalty-free license to the team's work.
- Your license extends to your Student Company. That's a company you founded or co-founded, or one in which students on the project together hold at least 25% of the voting equity. For a partner project, no competitor of the partner may control it.
- Course work stays non-exclusive. What your company builds afterwards is its own.
- Teammates who start separate companies each keep their licenses.

### Why investors want exclusive rights

Investors back companies that others can't easily copy, so they usually ask founders to assign their IP to the company. Licenses held by teammates or a partner weaken that position. Patents raise the stakes, because a patent's value is the right to exclude others. A co-owner outside the company can license competitors, and can block enforcement by refusing to join a lawsuit.

## Planning ahead

The agreements don't decide what happens if some of your team want to build a company on the project's work and others don't, or if Partner owns the work and leaves it unused. That's for you to settle. The Course recommends that your team agree on something early in the project, in writing, with Partner if there is one. It's far easier before anyone knows whether the work is valuable, or who will want to use it.

Why it matters:

- **A teammate moves on.** Three of you start a company and want to raise money. The fourth has taken a job elsewhere and won't sign anything. They keep their share and their license, so the company can't get the exclusive rights that investors usually want.
- **Partner shelves the work.** Partner owns the work, then decides not to use it. You can still show it in your portfolio, but no one else can build on it unless Partner agrees. If Partner holds a patent on it, no one else may use the invention.

Common approaches:

- **Assign at founding.** Everyone who wants in assigns their rights to the company when it's formed, in exchange for equity.
- **A buyout.** Teammates who don't join assign their rights to the company for a fair price agreed in advance, such as fully vested equity matching what founders or advisors get, or cash.
- **All in or all out.** Each teammate either joins the company or releases their rights to it.
- **A right to purchase unused work.** If Partner will own the work, ask, when the Project Agreement is signed, for a right to buy it if no one uses it for a set time, for example for Partner's costs plus a small amount of equity.

[The Course offers optional add-on forms for a teammate buyout and for a right to purchase unused work.] An add-on binds only those who sign it, and can't take away anyone else's rights, so it works best if everyone signs early. Write down what you agree. The instructor can point you to help, including patent attorneys' office hours and the Harvard Innovation Labs.

## Startups on openly licensed work

### Why it's usually easier

With a public license, there's nothing to deadlock: everyone may already use, change, and sell the work. Your company's value comes from what you add. You may license your own later code as you choose, if you keep the original notices. Adaptations of CC BY-SA material that you share must stay under CC BY-SA or a compatible license.

### Caveats

- **Others share the base.** Competitors and Partner can build on the same code.
- **Publishing is a public disclosure.** If anyone wants a patent, file before you publish.
- **Apache 2.0 licenses patents; MIT is silent.** Under Apache 2.0, each contributor licenses everyone under its patent claims that its contribution necessarily infringes. A recipient who sues, claiming the work infringes a patent, loses that license. So if you later patent what your Apache-licensed code does, its users may already be licensed. MIT doesn't mention patents, and whether it implies a patent license is unsettled.
- **Keep the notices.** MIT requires its copyright and permission notice in all copies or substantial portions. Apache 2.0 requires a copy of the license, notices in changed files, and keeping existing notices, including any NOTICE file. CC BY and CC BY-SA require credit and a license notice.
- **The project's name isn't licensed.** Apache 2.0 expressly grants no rights in trade names or trademarks, and Creative Commons licenses exclude trademarks. MIT doesn't mention them. Pick your own company name, and check that it's free.

> **Note for counsel:** please confirm that MIT's implied patent license is unsettled, and how dual licensing affects the Apache 2.0 patent license for a recipient who chooses MIT.

### FSL: a middle path

With a partner, the Functional Source License (FSL) is available only with joint ownership. A team without a partner may also choose it. It suits a team that expects to commercialize.

Anyone may use FSL code for any purpose except a "Competing Use." That means making the FSL code available to others in a commercial product or service that:

- substitutes for the code;
- substitutes for another product the owners offer using the code; or
- offers the same or substantially similar functionality.

This limits only uses of the FSL code itself. Internal use, and noncommercial education and research, are allowed. Each version becomes available under MIT or Apache 2.0 two years after it is released.

Under the agreements, any partner and each student may use the code for any purpose, including through a Student Company. That isn't a Competing Use. No owner may license the code on other terms without amending the agreement, and no owner owes the others a share of profits.

Weigh these points:

- Competitors are barred for two years, but your teammates and any partner aren't. Then each version is open to everyone.
- Until then, FSL code is source-available, not open source, and some users avoid it.
- Releasing FSL code is still a public disclosure.

## Harvard's IP policy

Harvard has approved the forms of the agreements for your Course, and a copy of its approval is attached to the course-level agreement.

Harvard's "Statement of Policy in Regard to Intellectual Property" applies to students. It covers anyone who uses Harvard funds, facilities, or other resources, or takes part in Harvard-administered research.

Ownership depends on how the work was made, not on who made it. Harvard owns inventions, software, and databases made with Harvard-administered funding, with more than incidental use of Harvard resources, or under a third-party agreement. If you invent with only incidental use, such as classrooms and instructional labs in ordinary course participation, you own the invention. Harvard keeps a limited right to use it for research and education.

### OTD's informal exception for coursework

OTD has created an informal exception for coursework. Harvard doesn't claim ownership of your rights in IP from your work in the Course, provided that:

- the Course isn't funded by, or conducted under, an agreement between Harvard and a third party; and
- the only Harvard-administered resources you use are those provided generally to everyone in the Course.

The instructor can tell you whether the exception applies to your Course.

### When it may not apply

- **Third-party agreements.** If a sponsor funds the Course for research, or provides enabling resources in exchange for IP rights, Harvard may own course inventions. You'd usually sign Harvard's Participation Agreement first.
- **Other Harvard resources.** Research computing, lab equipment, a research group's data, or Harvard-administered money can bring your work under the policy. That includes money spent only on supplies or computing fees. Ask the instructor first.
- **Research.** Harvard's rights from faculty-sponsored research come first. Ask before bringing lab work into your project.
- **People outside the team** may owe their work to an employer, which may then own their share.
