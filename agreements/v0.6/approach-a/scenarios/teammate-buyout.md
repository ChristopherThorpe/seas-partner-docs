**SCENARIO FOR COUNSEL: A TEAMMATE BUYOUT**

"CA" is the Course Agreement and "PA" the Project Agreement, as drafted in `agreements/v0.6/approach-a/`. Capitalized terms, such as Teammate, Student Company, Project Work Product, and End Date, have the meanings those agreements give them.

## 1. The decision for counsel

**The decision.** Should the agreements enforce a sensible, fair default buyout of an inactive Teammate, while making it easy for the students affected to choose anything else if they don't like it? Until counsel answers, the agreements set no default: CA 5.5 recommends a written agreement and allows an optional Add-On Agreement. The other options are an optional Add-On Agreement, or only a recommendation that each team agree on something. "Three options" below compares them.

**The owner's words.** The owner's first text, on November 10, which led to this paper. The owner later confirmed that it covers both defaults, the teammate buyout and the right to purchase unused work:

> I think both this and the partner buyback scenario need to be moved out as separate "scenario" documents to discuss with counsel about whether we want to force students to agree on those terms up front.
>
> I'm just not sure it's appropriate for the course to push students into that (versus providing them an addendum they can "add on" to the course documents and fits cleanly with the course documents.)
>
> I like showing them why it matters, and recommending that they agree on _something_, but I think creating a default for those things creates trouble.

The owner's follow-up set this paper's starting point:

> I think that's where we should start, with a decision for counsel to help us think through this here, and whether a sensible fair default is something we do want to enforce — while making it easier for them to do anything else if they don't like it.

**Counsel's help.** The owner asks counsel to help judge whether the drafted terms (Appendix 1) are fair enough to enforce, especially against a student who had to sign the Course Agreement to take the Course. Every student in the Course signs it, and some take the Course because their degree requires it. CA 2.4 tells them that no student is required to agree to an assignment of intellectual property to take the Course. A default buyout is a conditional assignment that every student signs (old CA 5.5(e)). It sits uneasily with that promise unless the way out is real.

## 2. The situation

Four students on a project own their work, and it has no public license. After the Course, three of them form a company and want to raise money. The fourth has taken a job elsewhere and won't sign anything (scenario 8 in `agreements/v0.6/SCENARIOS.md`).

Under the agreements as they now stand, each of the four keeps their rights in the work and a Teammate license from the others (CA 5.3). The founders' company may use the work through their licenses, and no one owes anyone an accounting. But no one can give the company exclusive rights. The fourth student keeps their share and their license, and only they can give them up (CA 5.3 and 5.5). CA 5.5 adds no default of its own.

Until round 6, the Course Agreement gave a default answer (old CA 5.5 and Exhibit B, Part 2). The company could buy out the fourth student's rights for fully vested equity at least equal to what the founders and part-time advisors received, or for cash at fair market value if the student refused the equity. That text is in Appendix 1. Appendix 2 rewrites it as a draft Add-On Agreement.

## 3. Why it matters

- **Students.** Without an agreement, co-owners can each use the work and grant non-exclusive licenses, so no one has exclusive rights (17 U.S.C. § 201(a); 35 U.S.C. § 262). The agreements keep that model: each student, and their Student Company, may use the work (CA 5.3). A Teammate who has moved on can stall a company just by not answering. Equally, a Teammate who did real work can be pressed to give up their share cheaply once the work looks valuable.
- **Partners.** A buyout doesn't touch the Partner License, so most Partners are indifferent to who among the students owns the work. A Partner may still care if a deadlock stops the students' company, which it may want to buy from, work with, or invest in.
- **Investors.** Investors usually want clean title: the company should own its core intellectual property, or hold exclusive rights in it. A former Teammate who keeps a license can use the work, including through their own Student Company. For a patent, a co-owner outside the company can also block enforcement by refusing to join a lawsuit. Due diligence will find the Course Agreement and ask who holds what.

## 4. Three options

### Option 1: a fair default with an easy way out

The drafted terms apply unless the students affected choose otherwise. The way out can be made easy:

- **A choice at signing.** The Project Agreement's cover page and the Project Election would add one line: "Later use among the students (check one): ☐ the default terms (default) ☐ none ☐ our own agreement, attached".
- **A later agreement wins.** Any later written agreement about the same question, signed by the students it affects, replaces the default for them.
- **Who may take it.** The choice at signing is the team's, and a later agreement needs both the company and the Teammate. If one student could opt out alone, the holdout would.

What already makes the drafted terms fair:

- The buyout comes only after the Teammate's End Date, and only if, on the date of the notice, they are not using the work commercially, personally or through a company.
- The Teammate may join the company instead, if both agree.
- The price floor is fully vested equity at least equal to the greater of what each founding student received for their own rights in the work and what the company's part-time initial advisors receive. Equity that founders earn by working for the company doesn't count, so the Teammate gets parity for the work already done, not for work they won't do.
- The Teammate gets cash at fair market value if they refuse the equity, or don't accept it within 30 days.
- There is no buyout while two or more groups of the team's students are developing the work, because there is no exclusivity to gain.
- The Teammate keeps the right to describe and show their own contribution in a portfolio or academic setting, without distributing it.
- The company must first promise to keep the Teammate's commitments, such as the Partner's license and confidentiality, and the Partner and the other students may enforce that promise.
- Notices go to the Teammate's Harvard and personal emails, with a text or voicemail, three times about [7] days apart, so the Teammate is likely to hear of the offer.

What may not be fair, or may not hold up:

- **A forced assignment.** The Teammate's rights pass when the company tenders the price, without the Teammate signing anything at the time.
- **The Teammate's own use ends.** The buyout ends all of the Teammate's rights in the work, including their Teammate license, except the portfolio and academic exception. Starting to use the work after the notice does not stop it.
- **The power of attorney.** If the Teammate won't sign recording papers within [15] business days, the company signs them in the Teammate's name.
- **Adhesion.** Every student signs the Course Agreement to take the Course, some in a required course, and some may be under 18.
- **The founders set the benchmarks.** Both comparisons in the floor depend on grants the founders choose, and the fair market value of startup equity is uncertain.
- **Money only.** A price dispute doesn't delay the assignment. The Teammate has only a claim for the difference.
- **Who pays a shortfall.** In the removed text, the company never promised to pay; tendering the price was only a condition of the assignment.
- **Judgment calls.** Whether the company is "actively developing" the work, and whether the Teammate is using it commercially, are facts the company judges first.

**Advantages.** It works even for a holdout who never signs anything again, because every student signed it at the start. Everyone knows the rule before choosing a project. The way out is meant to answer the owner's concern that the Course would be pushing students into these terms; whether it does is part of the decision.

**Risks.** The Course still writes a default on a question where its students' interests conflict, and most teams will keep it without reading it. The adhesion and under-18 questions remain. The choice at signing comes before anyone knows the work's value. The Partner also signs the Project Agreement's cover page, so it would be drawn into a question among students. A later change to the choice would then need a signed change under PA 6.3, unless the later-agreement rule applies. The default adds about 600 words to the Course Agreement.

Appendix 2 explains how its draft text would work as this kind of default.

### Option 2: an optional Add-On Agreement

A team opts in by signing an Add-On Agreement, such as the draft in Appendix 2. CA 5.5 already lets one fit: it binds only those who sign it, governs among them over the agreements on these questions, and cannot reduce anyone else's rights. CA 8.4 and PA 1.2 add that it is not a change to the agreements. The current drafts take this approach until counsel answers, with a bracket in CA 5.5 on whether the Course offers forms.

**Advantages.** It is voluntary, so the Course pushes no one. Teams can change it. The Course Agreement stays short.

**Risks.** It binds only those who sign. The holdout is the student least likely to sign, and an Add-On Agreement can't end the license of a Teammate who didn't sign it (CA 5.3 and 5.5). So it helps only if every Teammate signs early, before anyone knows who will leave. Forms the Course offers may need Harvard's approval. The § 204(a), securities, and tax questions remain for those who sign.

### Option 3: a recommendation only

The Student Guide explains why later use matters and recommends that each team agree on something in writing (its "Planning ahead" section). The Course offers no form.

**Advantages.** It is the simplest. There are no forms to approve or maintain, and the Course takes no position on the terms.

**Risks.** Most teams won't act until there is a dispute. Students without access to a lawyer fare worst, and terms will vary from team to team.

## 6. Open choices

- **An Add-On Agreement signed by only some Teammates.** It binds only its signers. A holdout who never signs keeps their rights and Teammate license, so the company still lacks exclusive rights. It works best if every Teammate signs early in the project. Under Option 1, the same gap would arise only if one student could take the way out alone.
- **When to sign.** Signing early is fairer, because no one yet knows the work's value or who will leave, but students may sign without much thought. Signing at founding is better informed, but by then the holdout may be gone.
- **Late joiners.** Whether a student who joins the project later may sign (bracketed in Appendix 2, Section 1).
- **Who may buy out whom.** If a company of any of the team's students may buy, the terms aren't mutual: a student who never signs can never be bought out, yet their company may buy out every student who did sign. That rewards holding out, which the add-on exists to cure. Section 2(b) of the draft brackets "the Parties" first.
- **The Teammate's own Student Company.** A Teammate's license extends to their Student Company (CA 5.3), and an Add-On Agreement that company did not sign can't end its license. The draft relies on the condition that the Teammate is not using the work through any company.
- **The power of attorney and its [15] business days.** Whether to keep them, or rely on the present assignment alone.
- **The Partner.** The Partner License is unaffected, so the draft doesn't need the Partner to sign. A Partner might still want to be told of a buyout.

## 7. Questions for counsel

1. **Enforceability** (from the note removed from old CA 5.5): whether the buyout is enforceable, including whether the present assignment satisfies 17 U.S.C. § 204(a) and 35 U.S.C. § 261 when the company may not yet exist, and whether the power of attorney is enforceable.
2. **Adhesion and required courses:** whether a default buyout binds a student who had to sign the Course Agreement to take the Course, especially in a course their degree requires; how it fits CA 2.4; and whether an easy way out (Option 1) answers that, and what would make it easy enough.
3. **Students under 18:** how they should sign, since an assignment or power of attorney they give could be voidable (see the note after CA 8.4).
4. **Securities and tax:** the treatment of the equity or cash a bought-out Teammate receives, and whether the company's grant needs a securities exemption.
5. **Forms:** whether forms the Course offers need Harvard's approval, and whether offering them amounts to giving students legal advice.
6. **The hook:** whether CA 5.5, with the sentences in CA 8.4 and PA 1.2 and 6.8 that make room for it, is enough for an Add-On Agreement to work, including one that assigns a student's rights or ends their Teammate license; and whether one signed by only some Teammates is workable.

### Course Agreement Section 5.5

**5.5 Buyout of an inactive co-owner.**

(a) This Section applies to Project Work Product that a team's students own and that has no public license. It does not apply to work under an FSL license or work a Partner owns.

(b) After a Teammate's End Date, a Student Company of one or more of the team's students that is actively developing the work may buy out that Teammate's rights in it. It may do so if, on the date of its notice, the Teammate is not using the work commercially, personally or through a company. Starting to use the work after the notice does not stop the buyout.

(c) If both agree, the Teammate may join the company instead.

(d) The company must offer the Teammate fully vested equity at least equal to the greater of (1) what each founding student received for their own rights in the work, and (2) what the company's initial advisors who don't work for it full time receive. Neither amount counts equity that vests as a founder works for the company. The company and the Teammate may agree on any other amount or form.

(e) The Teammate hereby assigns all of their rights in the work to the company, effective when the company tenders the price under Exhibit B. The assignment takes effect only if the company has first promised in writing to keep the Teammate's commitments about the work, such as the Partner's license and confidentiality. It ends the Teammate's rights in the work, except the right to describe and show their own contribution in a portfolio or academic setting, without distributing it.

(f) A dispute about the price does not delay the assignment. It is a claim for the difference only.

(g) A buyout is possible only while one group of the team's students is actively developing the work. If two or more groups are, there is no exclusivity to gain, so no one may use this Section, and each group keeps its license under Section 5.3 unless they agree otherwise.

(h) Exhibit B sets out the default process: what the notice must contain and show (including the founding students' and initial advisors' grants), the Teammate's 30 days to decide, cash at fair market value if the Teammate refuses equity, and signing recording papers for a Teammate who won't.

### Using this text as an opt-out default

The same text could serve as Option 1, a default with an easy way out, without a second full draft:

- **Who it binds.** Sections 2 and 3 would go back into the Course Agreement, as old CA 5.5 and Exhibit B, Part 2 were, and bind every Signer. Section 1 and the signature entries would go, because every student already signs the Course Agreement's roster.
- **How the way out is recorded.** The Project Agreement's cover page and the Project Election would carry the choice described under Option 1 ("the default terms", "none", or "our own agreement, attached"). One added sentence would say that the default does not apply among students who choose "none" or attach their own agreement, or who later sign a written agreement about the same question, which replaces it for them.

### Drafting choices in this draft

These are new, and need the owner's confirmation:

1. **Parties.** Only students sign. The Partner License is unaffected, so the Partner need not sign.
2. **"Party"** is used instead of "Signer", which the Course Agreement already defines.
3. **Late joiners** may become Parties (bracketed in Section 1).
4. **Who may be bought out, and who may buy.** The Teammate bought out must be a Party. Section 2(b) brackets whether the buying company must be a Student Company of Parties, or of any of the team's students as in old CA 5.5(b). 5. **Old CA 5.5(h)**, which pointed to Exhibit B, is dropped. Section 3 holds the process, and its opening sentence keeps the old Exhibit B's rule that the company and the Teammate may agree on something else.
6. **Enforcement** follows old CA 8.3 (a Student Company may enforce what benefits it) and old Exhibit B, Part 2, step 2 (the Partner and other students may enforce the company's promise).
7. **General terms** (law, signatures, age and voluntary signing, changes, "including", and severability) are written out from CA 8.4, so the form reads on its own.
