### Section 4. Legal principles: copyright, patents, and trade secrets

**What IP rules apply to the software we create?** Each team member who contributes to a piece of software, or determines the method steps that it performs, has an interest in it as an author, an inventor, or both. Team members' rights to allow or deny its use by others are equal. This does not necessarily mean that each person's financial stake is the same; that is subject to negotiation among the owners, and we have intentionally omitted financial terms from our course policies and approved Letter Agreement terms. Under the baseline Open Science model, everyone agrees to grant permissive public rights. Where projects do not follow the baseline, "owners" include students and industry partners.

**(a) Copyright.** Software is a copyrightable work. A person who contributes original material to a piece of software, which could include designs, specifications, or code, typically would be considered one of its "authors" under copyright law. Under US law, a co-author may use the work or grant non-exclusive licenses without the other co-authors' consent, but must account to them for their share of any profits. A co-author may also transfer their own share. An exclusive license or transfer of the whole work requires all co-authors' agreement.

> **Review note R-7 (copyright rules, for counsel):** please confirm the statement of US law in the paragraph above: co-authors may use the work or grant non-exclusive licenses without consent but must account for profits; a co-author may transfer their own share; and exclusive licenses or transfers of the whole work need all co-authors. Also confirm the startup example that follows. Section 8.1.1(d) and (e) of the Letter Agreement rely on this rule.

For example, you could use the work in a startup company that you found, but you would owe your co-authors their share of any profits, and you couldn't give the startup, an investor, or a buyer exclusive rights to the whole work without all co-authors' agreement. From this perspective, keeping lines of communication among teammates open (and cordial) as you work together will help you understand each other's aims and plan together how best to move forward. This is a key reason why the Course adopts Open Science (the MIT License) as its baseline, and the other options provide up-front clarity: by licensing the software explicitly from the outset, deadlocks among co-authors are eliminated, and, in most cases, all team members retain the permanent right to use the codebase.

As a technology professional, you should get into the habit of reviewing the terms and conditions of licenses that cover any open-source software or tools, such as generative AI tools, that you might use. (The terms of Harvard's licenses to generative AI tools provided to you may be more favorable than terms available to the general public.) In this way, you can make informed decisions about their use, based on the extent to which they might affect your complete ownership, or at least control, of the innovations you might make through their use. 

> **Review note R-2 (copyright and patent law, for counsel):** please confirm the statements of copyright and patent law in the next four paragraphs. In particular: a US co-owner may license non-exclusively and practice the patent without the other co-owners' consent and without accounting for profits (35 U.S.C. § 262); all co-owners generally must join a suit to enforce a US patent; the rules in other countries vary, and some require co-owners' consent to license or use; and the attorney of record's role in determining inventorship.

In the U.S., at least, a work is protected by copyright from the moment of its creation; however, you may also wish to consider registering the copyright in your code, which can provide additional protections. Note that there may be limitations or nuances applicable to copyrighting code created by generative AI.

**(b) Patent.** Sometimes a patent application may be filed to cover the methods carried out by a given software program. Unlike the copyright that may cover software, a patent may, to a limited extent, be commercialized by one of several patent co-owners working alone. In the U.S., a co-owner can license their interest in the patent without the other co-owners' consent, effectively creating a non-exclusive license without enforcement rights, because all co-owners generally must join a lawsuit to enforce a US patent. A US co-owner can also practice the patent commercially, for example by selling patented products and services through a start-up they found, and, unlike under copyright, generally owes the other co-owners no share of the profits. The rules for patents in other countries vary, and in some countries co-owners need each other's consent to license or even use the patent. Such a strategy has its limitations: non-exclusive licenses are a "tough sell" without the means to enforce the patent, and start-ups that do not hold all of the rights in their foundational IP may find it nearly impossible to attract investment. For these reasons, it is best for team members to work through various scenarios together as they work on their course project and, if anyone has commercial plans for their course IP, to try to build a business model that takes all inventors' rights into account.

The "attorney of record," that is, the person who prepares and files the application, is responsible for applying the legal standard of inventorship to those who have contributed to a given invention. Because students in the Course work collaboratively in teams, you should expect the attorney to consider each team member's and partner's contributions in making their legal determination of inventorship.

Assuming that no inventor owes an assignment to Harvard, the patent would be owned by all of the inventors. Again, Harvard's OTD exempts most student coursework from claims of IP rights, as described under "Does Harvard have any IP claims on my coursework?" in Section 2 of this Exhibit A.DS; however, if any inventor uses other resources, or involves anyone in the work who is not a student in the Course, it is possible that Harvard, a non-teammate individual, or an individual's employer (whether Harvard or another organization) might own their rights, potentially impeding a team's efforts to commercialize its course-related IP. For this reason, it is important for team members to exercise good "hygiene" in terms of both resource use and the involvement of people outside the team. For example, if you are a graduate student and incorporate into your coursework any research that could be subject to a participation agreement with Harvard, that research may be considered other "Harvard-administered resources." Please consult the course head and OTD about IP implications in more complex situations.

Finally, a "public disclosure" before a patent application is filed may affect or invalidate patent rights in the U.S. or other countries. This may include presentations at meetings; posters; abstracts; print or online publications or announcements of your invention or of results generated through its use; and pitches to potential investors, competition judges, or business partners. In projects governed by the open-source baseline, publication to a public repository is a public disclosure. Where a project is on an alternative proprietary track, no student should make a public disclosure (for example, by publishing any project materials on a public-facing website that does not require users to log in, or by posting project code to a public code repository) without the consent of all teammates, including any partner. Course events where Course Private Information is presented are ordinarily restricted to course participants and invited guests. The poster or video session is restricted to members of the SEAS community and invited guests. Not all attendees are bound by nondisclosure obligations, so responsibility for protecting any patentable inventions before these events rests with you, your teammates, and your partners. If your project may produce a patentable invention, talk to your partner, teammates, course staff, and legal advisors (if any) before presenting it at any course event. Until an application is filed, keep presentations to what your invention does and the results; don't describe how it works. A patent application (often a provisional application) may need to be filed first to preserve your rights, especially outside the US.

**(c) Trade secret.** Trade secret is a form of IP protection that applies only in limited circumstances, in which information key to a business is guarded by security measures commensurate in strength and scope with the importance of that information to the company's business interests. Because your work is being created in the context of a university class where other student groups and course staff see and respond to your work (that is, where there is an expectation of information sharing), and because of the open nature of academic research generally, we do not believe that trade secret protection is likely to apply to any IP that you create in the Course. This does not apply to partners' confidential information, which may include trade secrets protected under Sections 3.2 and 10.2 of the Letter Agreement.

### Section 5. Startups, commercialization, and licensing options

**What if some of us want to start a startup or join our partner as employees based on our project, but not everyone is going to join?** We strongly urge you to talk about and agree on how your project IP can be used by team members in the future. Under the baseline policy, releasing the project under a permissive open-source license (such as MIT or Apache 2.0) resolves this cleanly: anyone on the team can use the open-source code as the foundation for a startup or future commercial endeavor without needing to obtain consent from all teammates.

If your team has not opted into the baseline Open Science model, how commercialization works depends on the IP option your project chose under Section 8 of the Letter Agreement. Under copyright law, co-authors can't grant exclusive rights to the whole work without everyone's agreement. Under Option 8.1, an exclusive right is no longer available to anyone, so anyone can commercialize the IP according to its license. If Option 8.3 is selected, Partner obtains exclusive rights to the IP, so fewer parties need to agree on terms. If Option 8.2 is selected, investors and buyers could reject founders' nonexclusive licenses to the IP as insufficient and demand all co-authors transfer exclusive rights in the Project Work Product to the startup. In this case, any one stubborn co-author could refuse, so keeping communication open can help prevent downstream deadlocks. To address this up front, you might agree that any group of one or more authors or inventors could create a startup based on the project IP, in exchange for cash or equity compensation to non-participating co-authors. The instructor can help you find appropriate resources to evaluate your options and document your decision, including local patent attorneys who have agreed to hold office hours for interested students, and the Harvard Innovation Labs, which offer students free support.
